Why does the mean run into the tail?

With a right tail: mode, then median, then mean. Why that order?

Mode: if the 16 moved out to 30, would the mode change?

Mode: no. It only asks which value is most common, so it stays at the peak and ignores the tail.

Median: if the 16 moved to 30, would the median change?

Median: no. It counts values, not distances: 30 is still one value on the right. The tail's extra values put the middle just right of the peak.

Mean: and the mean?

Mean: jumps from 6.0 to 7.3. It is the balance point: each value pulls with its distance, like a child at the end of a seesaw.

Mode ignores the tail; median counts tail values; mean also counts how far they are. So the mean is dragged furthest. Flip the tail and it all mirrors.

Same data, different histogram

With 3 bins, how many groups of commuters do you see?

Now 12 bins: two groups were hiding. Too few bins hide groups.

So are more bins always better?

80 bins: random noise looks like bumps. Too many bins invent groups.

Then what is the right bin width?

There is no single right one. State the width you used, and try a few.

Solution: let one value stand in for everyone in a range: its midpoint. Some students score above it and some below, so the errors roughly cancel.

So the 16 students in 40–60 count as 16 × 50. Fill in every midpoint.

f × x estimates each range's total marks.

Divide the grand total by the number of students. It is a weighted mean: midpoints weighted by frequencies.

It is only an estimate: grouping threw away the exact marks.

L = lower limit of the median class (40), cf = cumulative f before it (14), f = its frequency (16), h = class width (20)

f₁ = modal class frequency (16), f₀ and f₂ = the classes before and after it

n = 50. Where is the middle student?

The 25th. Keep a running total of f: the cumulative frequency.

The running total passes 25 in 40–60: the median class.

Median = L + ((n/2 − cf) ÷ f) × h

= 40 + ((25 − 14) ÷ 16) × 20 = 53.75

Modal class: the highest frequency, 40–60 again.

Mode = L + ((f₁ − f₀) ÷ (2f₁ − f₀ − f₂)) × h

= 40 + (7 ÷ 9) × 20 ≈ 55.56

Mean 52.8 < median 53.75 < mode 55.56: a slight tail to the left.

Same average, different player

We need 30 runs to win today. Who do you send in?

Both batters average 40 runs over 12 innings.

Same average, yet you hesitated. How can we measure how reliable a player is?

Idea 1: range = highest − lowest. Simple, but it uses only two innings.

Idea 2: use every innings. How far is each score from the average 40?

Square each gap so + and − don't cancel, then average (divide by n − 1). That is the variance.

Runs² is an odd unit. Take the square root: the standard deviation, back in runs.

Send A. 40 ± 3 almost always clears 30. 40 ± 45 is a coin toss.

Why the spread recipe looks like this

Why not just average the gaps from the mean?

They always add up to zero: the + gaps cancel the − gaps. Squaring makes every gap count.

Note: the formula divides by n − 1, not n. That is the standard convention (textbooks, calculators, pandas). Why n − 1 opens our next module, inferential statistics.

Why take the square root at the end?

Variance is in squared units. The standard deviation is in the data's own units: "typically 3 runs from the average".

What does one standard deviation mean?

Batter A scores 40 ± 3. What does that ± 3 actually promise?

For bell-shaped data, about 68% of values lie within 1 SD of the mean.

About 95% lie within 2 SD.

About 99.7% lie within 3 SD. Beyond 3 SD is rare.

Batter A: about 95% of innings fall in 40 ± 6, i.e. 34 to 46. Almost always above 30.

Caution: only for roughly bell-shaped data. Batter B's lopsided scores don't follow it.

Comparing spread across different units

Which varies more across your class: marks out of 100, or CGPA out of 10?

SD: 12 marks vs 0.9 points. Marks vary 13 times more?

Unfair: marks sit on a scale ten times bigger. SD carries the units of the data.

Coefficient of variation

CV = (s ÷ x̄) × 100%

Marks: 12 ÷ 60 = 20%. CGPA: 0.9 ÷ 7.5 = 12%. Marks vary more, but 1.7 times, not 13.

You scored 72. Is that good?

It depends on everyone else. Here is the class of 20.

Count who scored at or below you: 14 of 20.

Percentile = % at or below you = 14 ÷ 20 = 70th percentile.

Quartiles cut the sorted class into four equal parts: Q1, Q2 (the median), Q3.

IQR = Q3 − Q1 = 74.75 − 57.25 = 17.5 marks: the spread of the middle half.

Which mark was your better performance?

You scored 62 in subject A and 75 in subject B. Which was your better performance?

Most say B, because 75 > 62. What else would you want to know?

How the class did. Class average: A 50, B 70. You beat A's average by 12, B's by only 5.

Is 12 marks above average a lot? Look at how spread out each class is.

In A, marks typically sit 6 from the average; in B, 10. Count your lead in typical gaps: A: 12 ÷ 6 = 2. B: 5 ÷ 10 = 0.5.

This count of typical gaps is the z-score. The typical gap is the standard deviation.

Your subject A mark was the better performance. Try other marks below.

z = (x − x̄) ÷ s

A: (62 − 50) ÷ 6 = 2.0
B: (75 − 70) ÷ 10 = 0.5

Same mark, same average, different spread

Two classes. Both average 60. You score 72 in both. Is it the same achievement?

Class X marks are tightly bunched (SD 6). Class Y marks are spread out (SD 15).

In which class is your 72 more impressive?

Class X: z = 12 ÷ 6 = 2.0. Only 1 of 40 scored higher. Class Y: z = 12 ÷ 15 = 0.8. 8 of 40 scored higher.

Same mark, same average. A bigger SD shrinks z; a smaller SD stretches it. A 12-mark lead is a big step in a tight class, a small one in a spread-out class.

Found an outlier. Now what?

The boxplot flagged 13 hours of screen time. Should we just delete it?

Second opinion, the z-score rule: for roughly bell-shaped data, |z| > 3 is unusual (outside 99.7%). Here z = (13 − 4.44) ÷ 2.57 ≈ 3.3.

Taming a long tail: the log transform

200 offers (illustrative): most 3–10 LPA, a few far out. The histogram is squashed to the left. Can we see the data better?

Take the logarithm. log₁₀(1) = 0, log₁₀(10) = 1, log₁₀(100) = 2: multiplying by 10 becomes adding 1. Big values are pulled in the most.

On the log scale the long tail pulls in, and the shape is close to a bell.

Before: mean 7.5, median 6.1 LPA. After: mean and median almost equal. The skew is gone.

Use logs for positive, right-skewed data: salaries, incomes, populations, file sizes. Only for values above 0.

100 students rate a course. No mean allowed. Can we still find the median and quartiles?

Two traps: temperature in kelvin is ratio (0 K means no thermal energy, so 300 K is twice 150 K). And differences of interval data are ratio: a 10 °C rise is twice a 5 °C rise.

Interval in engineering: gauge pressure

A gauge reads −0.5, 0, 1.5 and 3.0 bar. Is 3.0 twice the pressure of 1.5?

A gauge reads relative to the atmosphere. Its 0 means "same as the air outside", not "no pressure". That is why −0.5 (a partial vacuum) exists: gauge pressure is interval.

Absolute = gauge + 1.013 bar. Its 0 is a perfect vacuum, a true zero: absolute pressure is ratio.

Actual pressure: 4.01 ÷ 2.51 ≈ 1.6 times, not 2.

What you can say: 3.0 is 1.5 bar more, and it is twice the excess over atmosphere, which sets the net force on a tyre or pipe wall.

One variable: many ways to look

One column: 120 exam marks. Show every value as a dot: a strip plot.

Histogram: counts in bins. Shows the shape: here, two groups of students.

Density plot: a smoothed histogram. Same shape, no bins to choose.

Boxplot: the five-number summary at a glance. But it hides the two groups!

Violin plot: a boxplot wrapped in its density. Summary and shape together.

If the column holds categories, use a bar chart of counts.

Do two numbers move together?

90 students: more hours tends to go with higher marks. Can we put a number on "moving together"?

Draw lines at the average hours (5.2) and average marks (57.4). They split the plot into four boxes.

For each student, multiply (hours − x̄) × (marks − ȳ). Both above or both below average → positive (blue). One above, one below → negative (red). Here 80 of 90 are blue.

Average the products (÷ n − 1): the covariance. Here +44.0: positive, so they move together.

Measure study time in minutes instead. Same students, same picture, but covariance = 2641. Is it 60 times stronger?

cov(x, y) = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) ÷ (n − 1)

No. Covariance carries the units of both columns, so its size means little. Only its sign tells the direction.

Correlation: a unit-free measure

How do we turn covariance into a number that means the same in any unit?

Divide by both standard deviations. Hours vs marks: 44.0 ÷ (2.54 × 19.71) = 0.88, in hours or in minutes.

Sleep vs marks: r = 0.14. Weak: the points barely lean.

Screen time vs marks: r = −0.59. Moderate negative: more screen, lower marks.

Caution: this U-shape is perfectly regular, yet r ≈ 0. r only sees straight lines.

Sign = direction. Size = how tightly points hug a straight line: ±1 perfect, 0 none.

Pearson's correlation coefficient

r = cov(x, y) ÷ (sₓ · s_y)

Units cancel; r always lies between −1 and +1.

Same r, very different data

StatisticAll four

Correlation r0.82

Four datasets share these exact numbers. Do they look alike?

I: a noisy straight line. r describes it well.

II: a smooth curve. Perfectly patterned, but not straight, so r misses it.

III: a perfect line, plus one outlier dragging r down.

IV: no relationship at all, except one extreme point that creates r.

Anscombe's quartet (1973): always plot before you trust r.

TotalΣd² = 10

Two judges rank 8 student projects. Ranks are ordinal: is Pearson's r fair here?

Not quite: r assumes equal gaps. Spearman's ρ uses only the order: rank both columns, then compute r on the ranks.

ρ = 1 − 6Σd² ÷ (n(n² − 1))

= 1 − 60 ÷ 504 ≈ 0.88 (no ties). The judges largely agree.

Works on numbers too: does y rise when x rises? For 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32: Pearson r = 0.91 (not straight), Spearman ρ = 1 (always rising).

One extreme value can't dominate ρ: the top rank is only one step above the next.

Correlation is not causation

Attendance and marks: r = 0.65. Does attending class cause higher marks?

Maybe. But students who study more also attend more (r = 0.69). Study hours could drive both: a hidden confounder.

Ice-cream sales and drowning deaths rise together. Ice cream doesn't cause drowning: summer heat drives both.

Cities with more hospitals record more deaths. Hospitals don't cause deaths: city size drives both.

Other explanations: reverse cause (do good marks motivate attendance?) and chance, especially in small samples.

Correlation tells you where to look. Showing cause needs experiments, and the inferential tools coming next.

When a third variable flips the story

Kidney-stone treatments in a real study. Which would you choose?

TreatmentSmall stonesLarge stonesAll patients
A93% (81/87)73% (192/263)78% (273/350)
B87% (234/270)69% (55/80)83% (289/350)

Overall, B looks better: 83% vs 78%.

A wins in both groups but loses overall. How?

Small stones: A wins, 93% vs 87%.

A got the hard cases: 263 of its 350 patients had large stones, against 80 of B's. Stone size affected both treatment and outcome.

Large stones: A wins again, 73% vs 69%.

Simpson's paradox: before comparing groups, ask what else differs. Hence: three or more variables.

Source: Charig et al., BMJ 1986.

Three or more variables

Hours vs marks again. What if we also know each student's section?

Colour adds a third variable: section B scores higher for the same hours.

Size adds a fourth, attendance: a bubble chart. Already getting busy.

Correlation heatmap: every pair of number columns at once. Blue moves together; red moves opposite.

Pair plot: every pair as its own scatter, histograms on the diagonal.

One plot carries about 3–4 variables. Beyond that: heatmap, pair plot or small multiples.

From this sample to everyone

Our 90 students: hours studied vs marks, r = 0.88. Is that the truth about all students?

Our 90 are a sample. The population is every student who takes this course, now and in future years. We will never measure them all.

Imagine another batch of 90 from the same population, studying the same way. Its r is not exactly 0.88.

Draw more batches. Each gives a different r and a different average mark.
